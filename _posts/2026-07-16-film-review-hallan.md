Nothing can be smoother than reconstructing the memory of the Jeju April 3rd Incident. Unlike many other Democratization Movements in Korea, such as the May 18th Democratization Movement in 1980 Gwangju, the Jeju April 3rd Incident has not encountered much allegation of fabricated history from the political power or has not evoked public dispute. I believe there are basically two assumptions behind this appreciation of the Jeju Uprising: first, victims and perpetrators are obvious, and second, this incident is too isolated from the mainland people of South Korea. However, no incident can be categorized as black and white, and if it receives zero contention from South Korean society, I believe the effort to recognize this movement would be nullified since it means no interest in whatever is being said about the Jeju incident. The film director Ha Myung-mi attempts to achieve the double mission of increasing the sense of reality of the Jeju Uprising through the screen with a balanced view of complex historical events.

*Hallan* follows the story of Ajin and her daughter Haeseng on 1948 Jeju Island. The film starts with the scene of Ajin and village people heading to the mountain area. Ajin leaves Haeseng and her mother-in-law, believing that the military squad would not harm the elderly and children. However, it turns out that with an indirect approval from the U.S. officer in charge, the Korean army perpetrates a massacre of innocent civilians. After witnessing the death of her grandmother and neighbors, Haeseng goes up the mountain and suffers from aphasia due to this traumatic experience, while Ajin comes down to find her daughter, worried about her safety. Ajin meets three members of the mountain resistance and finds out that they actually killed Ajin’s husband, worrying that he would expose their presence when he went down to find his family. Ajin fortunately reunites with her daughter, and they escape into the cave. Knowing that the cave is connected to the other side of Jeju Island, they finally reach the seashore, only to be caught by the police and categorized as rioters. 	 

The film attempts to depict that the characters and groups are not monolithic. The mountain guerrilla unit is one example. While it is all too simple to say that the army and perpetrators killed innocent people, such violence also occurs among refugees and resistance groups. On the other hand, Private Moon is conscious of killing the innocent people. In the end, Moon cannot bear what he and his unit have done and kills everyone, including himself. 

Historically, the groups of military forces and the mountain guerrillas of Jeju were complex entities. The socialists played a leading role among the uprising groups, and often this fact disrupted the lives of Jeju people. The North Korean government honored the first leader of the movement, Kim Dal-sam, in the year just after April 3rd. The second leader, Lee Deok-gu, who took over from Kim, was also a socialist and eventually escalated the violent situation in Jeju, which presumably serves as the background for *Hallan* and led to more innocent casualties. From the government side, the Northwest Youth Association, a group of Northwest Korean people who came to South Korea due to persecution by bourgeois, Christians, and some far-right gangsters, actively participated in the massacre. Private Moon’s prayer as a Christian in the film seems to reflect this background, and their cruelty was directed not only at the Jeju people but also at the right-wing individuals who disagreed with them. During this period of purge and violence, the Jeju people, along with innocent civilians like Ajin and Haeseng, became victims of the era.

While we can understand the madness of ideology in this context, the film is too didactic in repeating “for a better world” from every character. In the film, every group of people says they hope for a better world for their descendants, or they are working to build a better Korea. Probably the director wants to leave it as an open question, since she does not show the exact ending of Ajin and Haeseng. Unfortunately, the director Ha constructs multi-dimensional situations at the expense of making flat characters. It is obvious that those characters lack distinctiveness, and from here the narrative itself becomes simple. When Ha constructs the memory of the tragedy, the repeatedly said phrase makes the movie more like a story of fabrication, even though the narrative is actually largely based on the oral history collection.

In addition, it would be better if the mise-en-scène emphasized the beauty of Jeju; that would help with the irony of this incident. The place of Jeju Island has a unique position in that it has different weather compared to the Korean Peninsula, and focusing on this particular geographical backdrop may help the audience understand why they had to go deep inside the mountain rather than simply leaving it as a background. Of course, such an approach carries the danger of romanticizing nature rather than focusing on the historical narrative, but I find myself wishing that Jeju’s unique natural environment had been used to visually amplify the tragedy. 

At the 25th New York Asian Film Festival, the small exhibition on the Jeju April 3rd Incident was installed at Film at Lincoln Center with the help of the Jeju 4·3 Peace Foundation. It is meaningful to introduce *Hallan* and *My Name*, which deal with the Jeju Incident in film curation, and to provide historical background, how it was represented in literature and films, and how scholars and activists have worked to record the reality. The thing is, for Yang Yong-hi, for example, the producer of *Hallan* and director of the documentary *Soup and Ideology*, this incident also lies in the memory of Zainichi who returned to their hometown of Jeju from Japan, only to unintentionally go back to Japan. The effort to treat this incident with the complexity it deserves does not end within the film, filling in what Hallan alone could not hold. The work of revisiting this tragic memory continues here, beyond the film, in New York, a multicultural space that gives this continuation its own meaning.
